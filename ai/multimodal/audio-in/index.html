Listening models

A waveform is the least token-shaped signal in the zoo: it is continuous, it has no natural frame boundaries, and the same sentence can be spoken in a whisper, a shout or a monotone without changing a word of it. A model that listens has to choose between two front ends. It can run a speech recogniser first and feed the trunk a transcript — cheap, readable, and deaf to how the words were said — or it can learn a vocabulary of audio tokens and put them straight into the trunk, so the model hears the sigh as well as the sentence. This part builds both routes and measures the difference.

1

Audio as input

A time-frequency picture

Nothing in a transformer wants a waveform. The conventional first move is the one signal processing has used for decades: cut the samples into overlapping windows, take a short-time Fourier transform of each, and stack the magnitude spectra into a spectrogram. Warp the frequency axis onto the mel scale, which matches how hearing allocates resolution, and the result is a two-dimensional picture — time along one axis, frequency along the other, energy as brightness. That picture is what audio encoders consume: a mel spectrogram of a 16 kHz signal at a 25 ms window and a 10 ms hop is a hundred frames per second, each a short vector of mel bands.

The sliders change the analysis window and the hop, which decide the trade every spectrogram makes: a long window resolves frequency finely but smears time, a short one does the reverse, and the number of frames per second of audio — the token count a native model will eventually pay — is set entirely by the hop.

A mel spectrogram of a chirp plus a tone, computed with GenMedia.dsp.melSpectrogram and drawn with Guide.drawHeatmap. The rising band is the chirp; the straight line is the tone.

The encoder route

Hear words, then read them

The encoder route is the pipeline this site already treats in full elsewhere: a trained speech recogniser consumes the spectrogram and emits text, and the trunk receives that text as ordinary subwords. It is the cheapest possible interface to a language model, because the sequence it produces is tiny — a few subword tokens per second of speech, compared to the hundreds of frames the recogniser itself looked at. It is also lossy in a specific and important way. A transcript preserves what was said and discards how it was said: pitch, loudness, tempo, hesitations, laughter and the identity of the speaker are all squeezed out by a model trained to be invariant to exactly those things.

The diagram shows the route end to end. Everything to the left of the transcript is recogniser work, and everything to the right is ordinary language modelling; the interface between them is a string.

The encoder route: waveform, mel spectrogram, recogniser, transcript, trunk. The transcript is a handful of subword tokens per second; the spectrogram underneath was a hundred frames per second.

The recogniser is a strong, separately trained model. Using it as the interface means the trunk inherits its errors and its invariances, and never sees the signal it threw away.

⚠ Text is a bottleneck with a loss function. An automatic recogniser is optimised to output the correct words, so it is trained to erase the paralinguistic channel. Any model built on its transcript has already lost emotion, emphasis and speaker before the trunk sees a single token.
3

The native-token route

Give the trunk a vocabulary for sound

The native-token route replaces the recogniser with a neural audio codec: an encoder maps the spectrogram to a small number of discrete tokens per frame, and a residual vector quantiser supplies the vocabulary. The token rate is a choice, not a property of the signal. A codec at 75 frames per second with eight residual codebooks emits 600 tokens per second; drop to 50 frames and four books and it is 200. The bitrate follows the same arithmetic, and the codec's job is to keep the audio recognisable at whatever rate you pick.

Those tokens go into the same trunk as the text, through an audio embedder, which is what makes a model native rather than cascaded. The bars compare the token rates of the three front ends on the same axis: subword transcripts, mel frames and codec tokens. Native audio is one to two orders of magnitude more tokens than the transcript for the same second of speech, and in exchange the trunk can hear prosody, background sound and speaker identity because nothing discarded them.

Tokens per second of speech for the three front ends. Codec rates come from GenMedia.dsp.tokenRate and the bitrate from GenMedia.dsp.bitrateKbps.

💡 The two routes are not rivals so much as a dial. A transcript is the cheapest way to get meaning into a language model; native tokens are the only way to get sound. Models that do both often run a transcript for reasoning and audio tokens for delivery, which is close to the thinker-and-talker split of the omni models in the next part.
4

Paralinguistics

What the words do not carry

Paralinguistics is everything about speech that is not the words: pitch and its movement, loudness, speaking rate, pauses, laughter, sighs, and the voice characteristics that identify a speaker. A recognition model is built to be invariant to all of it, so a cascade literally cannot represent it. A native audio model can, because the prosodic information is still in the tokens it was given — but "can" is doing work. Whether the model uses that channel depends on the data it was trained on, and on whether anyone asked it to.

Speech instruction following is the task that tests this. Give the model a spoken question and ask it to answer in a particular tone, or have it describe the emotion in a recording, or whisper back, and a transcript-only model fails at the first hurdle because the instruction is in the delivery. The contours below are a schematic of the channel: two prosodic tracks over one utterance, flattened as the expressiveness slider goes down, until what is left is barely more than a transcript in the shape of a curve.

Two prosodic contours over one utterance — pitch and energy — from a seeded stream, with the expressiveness slider flattening both. A transcript keeps the words and discards every point on these lines.

⚠ Native tokens are necessary, not sufficient. Having prosody in the sequence does not mean the model attends to it. Systems that answer spoken questions with tone and emphasis are trained on data where tone matters, and the same architecture trained only on transcription-style pairs will happily ignore the channel it was given.
5

Where this shows up

Recognition is one use of a listening model

From scratch

Recognisers trained on audio alone

The pure recognition task is the domain of models trained end to end on audio and transcripts, with no language model in the loop: frame classifiers with a CTC objective, transducers, and encoder-decoder recognisers such as Whisper. That treatment, and the streaming and duplex machinery built on top of it, lives in the audio volume.

Instruction following

Spoken questions, spoken answers

A multimodal trunk that accepts audio tokens can be asked questions about a recording, told to summarise a meeting, or handed a spoken instruction about the sound in front of it. The transcript interface is a special case of this, and a lossy one, which is why the interesting systems keep the audio tokens rather than only their words.

Once a model can hear and speak in the same token space, the remaining problem is timing: two streams have to be produced and consumed at once, the user has to be interruptible, and the reply has to start inside a few hundred milliseconds. That is the real-time loop, and it is the last part of this volume.

Further reading

These papers cover the two front ends: self-supervised encoders that learned speech representations before recognition, the recogniser that made attention the default, and the audio token models that let a language model hear the signal itself.

  • Alexei Baevski, Henry Zhou, Abdelrahman Mohamed and Michael Auli, "wav2vec 2.0: A Framework for Self-Supervised Learning of Speech Representations", 2020 — pretraining an encoder on raw audio before any transcript exists.
  • Wei-Ning Hsu, Benjamin Bolte, Yao-Hung Hubert Tsai and coauthors, "HuBERT: Self-Supervised Speech Representation Learning by Masked Prediction of Hidden Units", 2021 — discrete speech units learned without text, the ancestor of codec tokens.
  • Alec Radford, Jong Wook Kim, Tao Xu and coauthors, "Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision", 2022 — Whisper, the encoder-decoder recogniser whose output is the encoder route's transcript.
  • Zalán Borsos, Raphaël Marinier, Damien Vincent and coauthors, "AudioLM: a Language Modeling Approach to Audio Generation", 2022 — audio and speech as token streams a language model can predict.
  • Yunfei Chu, Jin Xu, Xiaohuan Zhou and coauthors, "Qwen-Audio: Advancing Universal Audio Understanding via Unified Large-Scale Audio-Language Models", 2023 — an audio-language trunk and the instruction-following tasks a transcript interface cannot express.
