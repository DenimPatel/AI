Alignment: RLHF, reward models, and DPO

SFT teaches a model to produce a plausible response in the right format. It has no mechanism for the much more common real situation: two responses are both reasonable, but one is clearly better — more helpful, more honest, better organized, less likely to be misused. That comparative judgment is what this stage, broadly called alignment, is built to train on, using human (or model) preferences rather than single correct answers.

1

Preference data: comparisons, not corrections

Where alignment data comes from

Instead of writing "the correct answer," labelers (human annotators, or sometimes another model acting as a judge) are shown a prompt with two or more candidate responses and asked which is better — a much easier and more reliable judgment to make than authoring an ideal response from scratch. Each such judgment becomes one training example: a chosen response and a rejected response for the same prompt.

2

Training a reward model

Method 1 of 2 — the RLHF route

The classic RLHF recipe first trains a separate reward model: a copy of the language model with its output head replaced by a single scalar score, trained so that it scores the chosen response higher than the rejected one for the same prompt. The standard loss (from the Bradley-Terry model of pairwise comparison) is:

L = − log σ( rθ(x, yₔ) − rθ(x, yₛ) )   yₔ = chosen response, yₛ = rejected response

Drag the margin below (how much higher the chosen response scores than the rejected one) and watch how the loss responds — this is the same sigmoid-based loss shape used by DPO later on this page, which isn't a coincidence.

3

RLHF: using the reward model to update the policy

Method 1 of 2, continued

With a trained reward model in hand, the SFT model (now called the policy) is optimized with reinforcement learning — typically PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) — to generate responses that score higher under the reward model. Crucially, a KL-divergence penalty against the original SFT model is added to the objective, discouraging the policy from drifting too far from sensible language just to farm reward-model score:

maximize  E[ rθ(x, y) ] − β · KL( πₖₕ₋ₜₜ₋ ‖ πₔₖₔ )
SFT model (policy)→ generate response→ score with reward model→ PPO update, penalized by KL to SFT reference→ repeat
⚠️ Why this is hard in practice: RLHF via PPO involves keeping multiple models in memory at once (policy, reward model, and a frozen reference copy for the KL term), is sensitive to hyperparameters, and can suffer from reward hacking — the policy finding ways to score well on the reward model that don't reflect genuine quality (e.g. padding responses, because the reward model happens to associate length with thoroughness).
4

DPO: skipping the reward model entirely

Method 2 of 2 — the simpler route

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) makes an important observation: for the KL-regularized reward-maximization objective above, the optimal policy has a closed form in terms of the reward function — which means you can algebraically substitute that relationship back into the reward-model loss and get an objective that trains the policy directly on preference pairs, with no separate reward model and no reinforcement learning loop at all:

L = − log σ( β · [ log(πθ(yₔ|x)/πₜ₋ₛ(yₔ|x)) − log(πθ(yₛ|x)/πₜ₋ₛ(yₛ|x)) ] )
SFT model (reference, frozen)→ SFT model (policy, trainable)→ compute DPO loss directly on (chosen, rejected) pairs→ standard gradient update

No reward model to train, no PPO rollouts, no separate reward-hacking failure mode against a proxy model — just supervised-style training on preference pairs, which is far closer in engineering complexity to the SFT stage from Part 4 than to a full RL pipeline. This simplicity is a large part of why DPO (and its variants) has become widely adopted since its introduction.

💡 OLMo's actual recipe: Ai2's Tulu 3 post-training pipeline for OLMo combines SFT, then DPO on preference data, then a further RL stage using verifiable rewards (RLVR) — rewarding correctness directly for tasks like math and precise instruction-following, where "correct" can be checked programmatically rather than judged by a trained reward model. It's a concrete, published example of mixing these techniques rather than treating them as mutually exclusive.
